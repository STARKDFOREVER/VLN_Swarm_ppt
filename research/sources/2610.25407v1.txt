AeroDASH: Asynchronous Vision-Language-Action Control for Continuous UAV Flight

Onboard inference latency creates a mismatch between the trajectories predicted
by vision–language–action (VLA) policies and the ongoing motion of unmanned
aerial vehicles (UAVs).
We present AeroDASH, an asynchronous VLA framework for continuous
language-conditioned flight. A B-spline policy is initialized through supervised
fine-tuning on expert demonstrations, providing continuous trajectory references
in position and yaw. During execution, trajectory fusion removes the expired
prediction prefix and connects the remaining segment to the active reference
with derivative continuity while limiting trajectory deformation.
To account for deviations induced by asynchronous execution,
execution-aware group relative policy optimization (GRPO) further adapts the policy
using trajectory-level rewards. On the UAV-Flow test set, AeroDASH achieves a
success rate of 76.56%, exceeding OpenVLA-UAV and AeroDASH w/o GRPO by 38.1 and
20.1 percentage points, respectively. Execution experiments show that AeroDASH
sustains motion during inference and reduces acceleration, jerk, and speed
fluctuations relative to asynchronous tracking of waypoint sequences.
We further demonstrate language-conditioned flight with onboard inference and
asynchronous trajectory execution. The code will be released upon acceptance.

I Introduction

Fig. 1: AeroDASH. The left panel illustrates language-conditioned flight in a real-world scene.
Asynchronous trajectory fusion connects delayed B-spline predictions to the
active reference. Execution-aware GRPO updates the policy using rewards from
realized asynchronous flights. Hollow markers denote B-spline control points.

Language-conditioned flight requires an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) to ground natural language instructions in visual observations and translate them into continuous three-dimensional motion. Vision–language–action (VLA) models have emerged as a promising paradigm for this task, learning to map visual observations and language commands directly to robot actions. However, continuous UAV flight imposes a stringent execution requirement that goes beyond discrete action prediction: the flight controller needs a temporally smooth reference between successive policy updates, with high-order derivative continuity across all degrees of freedom in the vehicle’s flat-output space.

Current VLA approaches for UAVs predict discrete waypoints or pose targets, leaving the system without a continuous reference between updates. Onboard inference latency further exacerbates this gap: during VLA inference, the UAV continues to move, so each delayed prediction is conditioned on a stale observation and arrives after part of its intended horizon has elapsed. Existing asynchronous execution strategies address this mismatch at the position level only, without enforcing the high-order derivative continuity that UAV flight controllers require across the flat-output space. Abrupt transitions in these derivatives produce large jerks that can destabilize the vehicle. A second gap lies in the mismatch between open-loop training and closed-loop execution. Supervised fine-tuning trains the policy to match individual predictions to expert demonstrations but does not directly evaluate the complete trajectory produced by asynchronous execution.

To overcome these challenges, we present AeroDASH (Fig. 1 ), an asynchronous VLA framework for UAVs that sustains continuous language-conditioned flight under onboard inference latency. To address the first gap, the VLA policy predicts control points that define a cubic B-spline over the quadrotor’s four-dimensional flat-output space, providing an analytically differentiable reference throughout each inference interval. When a delayed proposal arrives, the portion preceding the scheduled switch time is removed and the remaining segment is refitted to enforce high-order derivative continuity with the active reference. The fusion formulation enforces C^{2} continuity of the trajectory reference at switching boundaries while penalizing deviations from the retained prediction. To address the second gap, we introduce execution-aware post-training with group relative policy optimization (GRPO). We optimize the policy using trajectory-level rewards that measure agreement between the complete trajectories produced by asynchronous execution and expert demonstrations.

We design an asynchronous B-spline fusion mechanism that aligns delayed predictions with execution time and enforces C^{2} continuity at switching boundaries while limiting trajectory deformation.

We introduce GRPO post-training with trajectory-level rewards computed from asynchronously executed trajectories to improve agreement with expert demonstrations.

II Related Work

II-A Vision-Language-Action for Drones

General-purpose VLA frameworks learn language-conditioned robot actions from
diverse embodied demonstrations [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . For UAVs, existing work spans long-horizon
language-guided navigation and fine-grained flight control.
AerialVLN [ 5 ] studies navigation toward a distant goal,
whereas UAV-Flow Colosseo [ 6 ] pairs individual flight
instructions with pilot demonstrations and trains VLA policies to predict
UAV poses. Its Flying-on-a-Word tasks require the flight path to follow the
spatial relationships specified by the instruction, making the executed path
relevant in addition to the final position.

Reinforcement learning extends VLA training beyond demonstration imitation.
SimpleVLA-RL [ 7 ] applies outcome-driven reinforcement
learning to OpenVLA-OFT, using online trajectory sampling and exploration to
improve manipulation performance and generalization. Its formulation provides
feedback on the consequences of sequential actions, rather than supervising
each prediction solely against demonstrated actions. In aerial navigation,
EG-GRPO [ 8 ] augments online rollouts with expert data to guide
exploration and improve alignment with fine-grained instructions.

These studies establish reinforcement learning as a means of improving
instruction-conditioned behavior, while AeroDASH focuses on the effect of
asynchronous execution on the resulting flight trajectory. The policy predicts
B-spline control points and receives GRPO updates from closed-loop trajectory
returns. This training objective evaluates successive predictions through
their execution outcomes, complementing the temporal alignment and trajectory
fusion discussed next.

II-B Asynchronous Trajectory Execution

Optimization-based trajectory generation provides a foundation for continuous
UAV flight. Minimum-snap methods [ 9 ] penalize
high-order derivatives of polynomial trajectories to generate smooth motion
through prescribed waypoints. Richter et al. [ 10 ] develop an efficient formulation for jointly optimizing polynomial segments
and allocating their durations. MINCO [ 11 ] further supports
efficient trajectory optimization under geometric and dynamic constraints.
These methods optimize flight trajectories from planning objectives and
constraints; AeroDASH instead uses optimization to correct a trajectory
predicted by a language-conditioned policy while limiting changes to its shape.

Receding-horizon planners update trajectories as the flight state evolves.
B-spline-based systems exploit local support and analytic derivatives for
online kinodynamic replanning [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] , while FASTER [ 16 ] maintains a committed trajectory during successive planning cycles. These
mechanisms allow flight to continue during planning. For a VLA, however, a
prediction is conditioned on an earlier visual observation and may arrive
after part of its intended execution interval has elapsed. Maintaining an
executable trajectory during inference therefore does not by itself resolve
how the delayed prediction should be aligned and connected to it.

RTC and REMAC address asynchronous execution through action generation.
RTC [ 17 ] guides new predictions using overlapping actions,
whereas REMAC [ 18 ] learns delay-aware corrections and preserves
the executed prefix during sampling. Both promote consistency between
successive predictions, but neither explicitly imposes velocity and
acceleration matching at the switching boundary.

AeroDASH aligns delayed B-spline predictions with execution time and refits
the remaining curve to match the current reference’s position, velocity,
and acceleration while limiting deformation. GRPO post-training further
adapts the policy to asynchronous execution using closed-loop trajectory returns.

III-A Preliminaries

AeroDASH uses an autoregressive OpenVLA policy [ 2 ] to generate B-spline trajectory proposals. At query time t_{q} , the policy receives o_{q}=(I_{q},\ell,\mathbf{s}_{q}) , where I_{q} is a forward-facing RGB image, \ell is the flight instruction, and \mathbf{s}_{q}=[x_{q},y_{q},z_{q},\psi_{q}]^{\top} contains the UAV position and yaw.
Position and yaw form a set of flat outputs for the quadrotor
model [ 19 ] . We parameterize trajectories in this
four-dimensional output space.
The policy generates 28 action tokens \mathbf{y}_{q}=(y_{q,1},\ldots,y_{q,28}) according to

\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{q}\mid o_{q})=\prod_{j=1}^{28}p_{\theta}\!\left(y_{q,j}\mid o_{q},\mathbf{y}_{q,<j}\right), (1)

where \theta denotes the policy parameters and \mathbf{y}_{q,<j} contains
the preceding action tokens. Detokenization and denormalization map these
tokens to seven four-dimensional B-spline control points \mathbf{C}_{q,i}^{b}=[x_{q,i}^{b},y_{q,i}^{b},z_{q,i}^{b},\psi_{q,i}^{b}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4} , i=1,\ldots,7 .
Superscript b denotes a local frame centered at the UAV position and aligned
with its heading at t_{q} , with the vertical axis unchanged. Adding the fixed
control point \mathbf{C}_{q,0}^{b}=\mathbf{0} yields eight controls that
jointly parameterize a continuous flat-output trajectory.

Let \mathbf{C}_{q,i} denote the corresponding world-frame controls, obtained
using the coordinate transformation in Sec. III-B .
These controls define a clamped cubic B-spline with uniformly spaced interior
knots [ 13 , 15 ] :

\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{q}(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{7}N_{i,3}(u)\mathbf{C}_{q,i},\qquad u\in[0,1], (2)

where N_{i,3} denotes the cubic B-spline basis function. The normalized phase u corresponds to physical time t=t_{q}+uT_{H} , where T_{H}>0 is the proposal
duration.

III-B Continuous Trajectory Generation

To learn continuous flight trajectories from demonstrations, the VLA needs
supervision in its control-point output representation. We fit B-splines to
expert flight segments and use the fitted control points as prediction targets.
We then train the policy through SFT using low-rank adaptation (LoRA).
Each demonstration segment contains M=8 flat-output samples, expressed in the
query-local frame defined in Sec. III-A and arranged as \mathbf{S}_{q}^{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 4} . For spline fitting, we map the sample
indices to [0,1] using u_{m}=(m-1)/(M-1) . Before fitting, yaw angles are
unwrapped to avoid artificial discontinuities at the angle wrap boundary. Let \bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 7} have entries \bar{\Phi}_{m,i}=N_{i,3}(u_{m}) , i=1,\ldots,7 . With \mathbf{C}_{q,0}^{b}=\mathbf{0} , the seven control-point targets are obtained
by least-squares fitting:

\mathbf{C}_{q,1:7}^{b,*}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times 4}}\left\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{S}_{q}^{b}\right\|_{F}^{2}. (3)

The rows of \mathbf{C}_{q,1:7}^{b,*} correspond to the seven predicted
control points.

The fitted control points are normalized and tokenized to form the target sequence \mathbf{y}_{q}^{*} . The action-token prediction loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}=-\frac{1}{28}\sum_{j=1}^{28}\log p_{\theta}\left(y_{q,j}^{*}\mid o_{q},\mathbf{y}_{q,<j}^{*}\right). (4)

At inference, the decoded controls are transformed into the world frame using
the query position \mathbf{p}_{q}=[x_{q},y_{q},z_{q}]^{\top} and yaw \psi_{q} :

\begin{aligned} \mathbf{p}_{q,i}^{w}&=\mathbf{R}_{z}(\psi_{q})\mathbf{p}_{q,i}^{b}+\mathbf{p}_{q},\\
\psi_{q,i}^{w}&=\psi_{q,i}^{b}+\psi_{q},\end{aligned}\qquad i=0,\ldots,7. (5)

Here, \mathbf{p}_{q,i}^{b} is the position component of the local control point,
and \mathbf{R}_{z}(\psi_{q}) denotes rotation about the vertical axis. The
transformed controls \mathbf{C}_{q,i}=[(\mathbf{p}_{q,i}^{w})^{\top},\psi_{q,i}^{w}]^{\top} define the
world-frame proposal in ( 2 ).

III-C Asynchronous Trajectory Fusion

Fig. 3: Asynchronous trajectory fusion over three successive predictions.
For prediction i , inference starts at t_{q,i} and the result becomes
available at t_{r,i} ,
while the current reference continues to execute until the scheduled switch
at t_{h,i} . The gray dashed prefix expires before the switch; the remaining
prediction (orange) is corrected to form the fused reference (green),
retaining its original end time t_{q,i}+T_{H} . The bottom row shows the
resulting reference across switches. Green dashed boxes indicate preparation
windows.

SFT learns expert trajectories but does not enforce continuity between delayed
predictions and the reference being tracked. Since the UAV keeps moving during
inference, the returned prediction must first be aligned with the scheduled
switch time. Temporal alignment alone does not resolve differences in the flat
outputs and their first two physical-time derivatives at the switch.
Fusion therefore adjusts the remaining prediction to match this boundary,
avoiding abrupt changes in the reference sent to the controller.

A query at t_{q} produces \hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{q} at t_{r} , after an
inference delay of t_{r}-t_{q} (Fig. 3 ).
The UAV continues tracking the current reference \boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{act}} throughout inference and preparation,
switching to the corrected proposal at t_{h} only if accepted.
The current reference must cover this entire interval.

\displaystyle t_{q} \displaystyle\leq t_{r}\leq t_{h}<t_{q}+T_{H}, (6) \displaystyle\Delta_{q} \displaystyle=t_{h}-t_{q},\qquad u_{q}=\frac{\Delta_{q}}{T_{H}}.

Here, \Delta_{q} includes inference and any further waiting before activation; u_{q} is the elapsed fraction of the prediction horizon.
By t_{h} , the part of the prediction assigned to [t_{q},t_{h}) has expired.
We remove this part and, for u_{q}<1 , keep the segment assigned to [t_{h},t_{q}+T_{H}] . In the prediction’s original time coordinate u , the retained
segment occupies [u_{q},1] .

To express the retained segment on the same [0,1] interval used by the spline
representation, we introduce v , with v=0 at t_{h} and v=1 at t_{q}+T_{H} :

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{q}^{+}(v) \displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{q}\!\left(u_{q}+(1-u_{q})v\right),\quad v\in[0,1], (7) \displaystyle T_{q}^{+} \displaystyle=(1-u_{q})T_{H}.

Here, T_{q}^{+} is the remaining duration and t=t_{h}+vT_{q}^{+} is the physical time.
Predictions with u_{q}\geq 1 have expired and are discarded.

We first fit the remaining prediction with eight B-spline control points
using regularized least squares. We translate all spatial controls by the
same offset so that the retained curve starts at the active-reference
position at t_{h} , without changing yaw or the remaining duration.
Let \mathbf{C}_{q}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times 4} denote these reanchored controls.
We then adjust them by solving a constrained
least-squares problem. At the switch, the corrected trajectory must match
the current reference in position, velocity, and acceleration, as well as yaw,
yaw rate, and yaw acceleration. The objective keeps the corrected curve close
to the fitted prediction and penalizes changes in its third position derivative
and second yaw derivative to limit deformation.

The boundary conditions require derivatives with respect to physical time.
For the original prediction, t=t_{q}+uT_{H} gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{q}}{\mathrm{d}t^{r}}=T_{H}^{-r}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{q}}{\mathrm{d}u^{r}},\qquad r\in\{1,2\}. (8)

For the refitted remaining segment, the same conversion uses its coordinate v and duration T_{q}^{+} . The current reference and its derivatives are
evaluated at t_{h} .

For candidate controls \mathbf{C} , let \mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{C}}(s) and \psi_{\mathbf{C}}(s) denote
position and yaw at local physical time s\in[0,T_{q}^{+}] , measured from t_{h} .
The corrected controls satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{q}^{\mathrm{exec}} \displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times 4}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fit}}\!\left(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{C}_{q}^{+}\right), (9) \displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{p}_{\mathbf{C}}^{(r)}(0) \displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{act}}^{(r)}(t_{h}),\quad r=0,1,2, \displaystyle\psi_{\mathbf{C}}^{(r)}(0) \displaystyle=\psi_{\mathrm{act}}^{(r)}(t_{h}),\quad r=0,1,2.

In ( 9 ), superscript (r) denotes differentiation
with respect to physical time.
These constraints provide C^{2} continuity at the trajectory switch.

The boundary conditions specify how the new trajectory starts but leave
freedom in the rest of the curve. We select the corrected trajectory by
penalizing changes to the fitted remaining segment:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fit}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{curve}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{deriv}}+\varepsilon\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}_{q}^{+}\|_{F}^{2}. (10)

Here, \lambda weights the derivative correction term, and \varepsilon controls control-point regularization.
The curve term measures weighted squared differences from the remaining
prediction, with greater weight on its later portion. The derivative term
penalizes changes in the third position derivative and second yaw derivative,
normalized relative to the curve term. These derivatives are taken with
respect to v . The final term penalizes changes to the control points.

At this projection stage, a fitted proposal that already satisfies the boundary
constraints remains unchanged because the objective penalizes only the correction.
The projection enforces boundary continuity while minimizing changes to the
retained trajectory.
Before activation, the executor checks speed, acceleration, and yaw-rate bounds using the spline’s
derivative control points. A candidate that passes these checks becomes the
active reference at t_{h} , with duration T_{q}^{+} .
Rejected proposals and missed execution deadlines invoke a brake-to-hover
fallback from the active-reference boundary. After braking, a stationary
reference is maintained until the episode terminates.

III-D Execution-Aware GRPO

Accumulated closed-loop deviations shift subsequent observations away from
the demonstration distribution used for SFT. We therefore adapt the
policy to asynchronous execution using trajectory-level rewards and a
GRPO-style update [ 20 ] .

For each task i , the pre-update policy \pi_{\mathrm{old}} generates K stochastic rollouts. We compute group-relative advantages from rollout returns
and use them in a token-level clipped objective. Pre-update log probabilities
are recorded for sampled action tokens associated with the executed trajectory.

We evaluate the complete executed trajectory against the expert demonstration
using both position and orientation. Dynamic time warping aligns the two
sequences to accommodate differences in execution speed. We use normalized
dynamic time warping (nDTW) [ 21 ] as the trajectory-level
reward, with higher values indicating closer agreement.
Let \boldsymbol{\rho}_{i}=(\rho_{i,1},\ldots,\rho_{i,M_{i}}) and \boldsymbol{\chi}_{i,k} denote the corresponding feature sequences of the
demonstration and rollout k , respectively:

\displaystyle\operatorname{DTW}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{i,k}) \displaystyle=\min_{W\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{(m,n)\in W}\|\rho_{i,m}-\chi_{i,k,n}\|_{2}, (11) \displaystyle L_{i} \displaystyle=\sum_{m=2}^{M_{i}}\|\rho_{i,m}-\rho_{i,m-1}\|_{2}, \displaystyle\operatorname{nDTW}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{i,k}) \displaystyle=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\operatorname{DTW}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{i,k})}{L_{i}}\right),

Here, \mathcal{W} contains sequence alignments that preserve temporal order,
and L_{i} normalizes the discrepancy by the demonstration’s path length in
the same feature space. At the end of rollout k , we assign R_{i,k}=\operatorname{nDTW}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{i},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{i,k}) .

We screen rollout groups to avoid policy-gradient updates based solely on
failed or non-improving exploration. A group is retained only when at least
one valid rollout exceeds a reference return threshold. The validity criterion
and threshold are specified with the training settings.

For each retained group, the trajectory advantage is

A_{i,k}=\frac{R_{i,k}-\bar{R}_{i}}{\max(\sigma_{i},\eta)}, (12)

Here, \bar{R}_{i} and \sigma_{i} are the mean and standard deviation of the
group’s returns, and \eta>0 sets a lower bound on the normalization
denominator. Both positive and negative advantages are retained. Each rollout’s advantage is shared by its action tokens used in
the GRPO update.

The policy objective increases the probability of sampled actions with
positive advantages and decreases it for those with negative advantages.
For each action token, we compare its probability under the current and
pre-update policies, conditioned on the same observation and preceding
tokens, denoted by h_{i,k,q,j}=(o_{i,k,q},\mathbf{y}_{i,k,q,<j}) .
Both probabilities are evaluated without sampling filters and at unit
temperature:

r_{i,k,q,j}(\theta)=\frac{p_{\theta}(y_{i,k,q,j}\mid h_{i,k,q,j})}{p_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,k,q,j}\mid h_{i,k,q,j})}. (13)

We retain PPO-style probability-ratio clipping [ 22 ] to constrain
policy updates, yielding the following GRPO-style objective:

\displaystyle\bar{r}_{i,k,q,j}(\theta) \displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}\bigl(r_{i,k,q,j}(\theta),1-\epsilon_{-},1+\epsilon_{+}\bigr), (14) \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta) \displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{i,k,q,j}\!\left[\min\!\left(r_{i,k,q,j}A_{i,k},\bar{r}_{i,k,q,j}A_{i,k}\right)\right].

The parameters \epsilon_{-} and \epsilon_{+} specify the lower and upper
clipping margins. The clipped policy loss is averaged over the action tokens used in
each prediction, then over predictions, rollouts, and tasks. Excluded groups
contribute zero to this loss.

To discourage excessive deviation from the pre-update policy, we add a
Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty against a frozen copy, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} .
The penalty is evaluated on observation and token contexts collected from
greedy reference rollouts, denoted by \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ref}} :

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}+\beta\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid h)\right). (15)

Here, \beta\geq 0 controls the KL regularization strength. Only the policy’s
LoRA adapters are updated; the backbone and reference-policy parameters
remain frozen.

IV-A Experimental Setup

IV-A 1 Dataset and Baselines

We evaluate all methods on 273 tasks spanning ten instruction categories in
the UAV-Flow simulation test set [ 6 ] . Initial conditions
and language instructions are identical across methods.

We compare AeroDASH with OpenVLA-UAV [ 6 ] , the
UAV-adapted OpenVLA baseline introduced in UAV-Flow. We additionally evaluate
a Waypoint Chunking baseline that predicts seven waypoints per query.
Both this baseline and AeroDASH produce seven four-dimensional outputs
per query, represented as waypoints and B-spline control points, respectively. This comparison controls the number of predicted outputs
when evaluating the action representation and execution method.

OpenVLA-UAV uses its native sequential executor. The waypoint-chunk baseline,
AeroDASH w/o GRPO, and AeroDASH all use asynchronous execution.
The waypoint-chunk baseline tracks the predicted waypoints sequentially.
AeroDASH w/o GRPO uses the supervised
policy with the same B-spline representation and trajectory-fusion executor
as AeroDASH, isolating the contribution of GRPO post-training.

IV-A 2 Training and Implementation Details

Both the waypoint-chunk baseline and the AeroDASH B-spline policy are trained for
50,000 SFT steps. GRPO post-training is initialized from the resulting B-spline
policy and uses 2,730 tasks from the UAV-Flow-Sim training set. During GRPO,
only the LoRA adapters are updated; the backbone and reference policy remain
frozen. Training uses six NVIDIA RTX 4090 D GPUs.
We optimize the LoRA adapters with AdamW at a learning rate of 10^{-5} ,
using rank 32 and \alpha=16 . GRPO uses clipping parameters \epsilon_{-}=0.20 and \epsilon_{+}=0.28 ,
and a KL penalty weight of 0.01. Each iteration
collects eight rollouts and one greedy reference rollout per task, with an
advantage normalization floor of \eta=0.006 .
Rollouts use temperature-scaled sampling with top- k and top- p filtering.

During GRPO rollouts and navigation evaluation, each predicted trajectory covers T_{H}=1.4\, s. We use a fixed inference delay of 0.6\, s and switch trajectories 0.7\, s after each query. The controller receives reference updates at 10\, Hz while inference runs asynchronously.

A rollout terminates when the policy consistently predicts negligible motion
and the executed trajectory settles.

The greedy rollout return provides the reference \mu_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}} for group selection. A group is retained for GRPO updates only if at least
one rollout completes normally and achieves R_{i,k}>\mu_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}+\eta .
Excluded groups remain in the task average with zero GRPO contribution; the
KL term is computed separately as defined in ( 15 ).

IV-A 4 Onboard Deployment

The UAV carries a Jetson Orin NX with 16 GB memory, a forward-facing RGB
camera, and a PX4 flight controller. Super-LIO [ 23 ] estimates the UAV state from a Livox MID-360 LiDAR and its IMU.
Images are resized to 224\times 224 for policy inference. Through ROS 2
and MAVROS, the system supplies odometry and continuous trajectory references
to PX4 at 50\, Hz, independently of policy inference.

The onboard policy directly predicts B-spline control
points. We use TensorRT for inference and merge
the LoRA adapters before model export.
Activation-aware weight quantization (AWQ) [ 24 ] reduces the language model to 4-bit weights and 16-bit activations (W4A16),
while the visual encoder and multimodal projector use mixed FP16/FP32
precision. The deployed policy has a mean inference latency of 672\, ms
on the Orin NX.

IV-A 5 Evaluation Metrics

We report success rate (SR) and nDTW [ 21 ] .
SR is the percentage of flights judged by manual review to satisfy the
language instruction. We compute nDTW using the UAV-Flow evaluator for all
methods, following the same feature representation and normalization used
for the training reward in ( 11 ).

We compute execution metrics from vehicle-state histories generated by the
dynamics simulation. Jerk is calculated by applying central differences directly
to the raw acceleration samples at 1\, ms intervals. For each condition, we calculate mean
speed \bar{v} , speed standard deviation \sigma_{v} , and the 95th-percentile
acceleration and jerk magnitudes a_{95} and j_{95} over [2.8,9.8)\, s.
Table 5 reports the equal-weight average of each
metric across all 40 conditions.

IV-B Quantitative Comparison

V Conclusion

We presented AeroDASH, an asynchronous VLA framework for continuous
language-conditioned UAV flight. Trajectory fusion aligns delayed B-spline
predictions with the active reference while maintaining derivative continuity
between successive updates. Execution-aware GRPO further adapts the policy to
closed-loop deviations through trajectory-level feedback. UAV-Flow experiments
demonstrate improved task success. In execution simulations, AeroDASH maintains
continuous motion with lower acceleration and jerk than waypoint-based execution.
Onboard flights further demonstrate language-conditioned control with policy
inference and trajectory execution running concurrently.
